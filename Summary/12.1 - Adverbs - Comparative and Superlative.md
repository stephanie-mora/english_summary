# Adverbs - Comparative and Superlative

## Introduction

An **adverb** is a type of word that describes how a verb is done and modifies a sentence to make it more specific. There are many different types of adverb, including:

- **Comparative Adverb -** A comparative adverb is used to compare two different verb actions. They usually show an increase or decrease in the action or that one thing is 'better' or 'worse' than the other verb action.

- **Superlative Adverb -** A superlative adverb is used to show the highest or lowest degrees of an action. They’re similar to superlative adjectives, which modify a noun to the highest or lowest degree.

Just like adjectives, **adverbs** can be compared. We use **comparative adverbs** to compare two actions, and **superlative adverbs** to compare one action with all others.

## Adverbs: Degrees of Comparison

Some adverbs can be used in three different degrees of comparison:

| Degree | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Positive Degree** | The base form of an adverb, without making any comparison | slowly, naturally, well, fast, hard |
| **Comparative Degree** | Compares the intensity of one action to another | more slowly, faster, better |
| **Superlative Degree** | Shows the highest/lowest intensity among three or more | the most slowly, the fastest, the best |

> **Note:** Only **gradable adverbs** (adverbs that can have different degrees of intensity) can be used in comparative or superlative forms. Non-gradable adverbs like *really, completely, totally* cannot be compared.



## Rules for Forming Comparative and Superlative Adverbs

### 1. Adverbs ending in -ly

With adverbs ending in **-ly**, you must use **more** to form the comparative, and **the most** to form the superlative.

| Adverb | Comparative | Superlative |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| fluently | more fluently | the most fluently |
| slowly | more slowly | the most slowly |
| quickly | more quickly | the most quickly |
| beautifully | more beautifully | the most beautifully |
| carefully | more carefully | the most carefully |

**Examples:**

- Kim speaks **more fluently than** Ali (does).
- Anna speaks **the most fluently of** all.
- The teacher spoke **more slowly** to help us to understand.
- Could you sing **more quietly** please?

### 2. One-syllable adverbs 

With short adverbs that do not end in **-ly** comparative and superlative forms are identical to adjectives: add **-er** to form the comparative and **-est** to form the superlative. If the adverb ends in *e*, remove it before adding the ending.

| Adverb | Comparative | Superlative  |
|--------|-------------|--------------|
| hard   | harder      | the hardest  |
| fast   | faster      | the fastest  |
| early  | earlier     | the earliest |
| late   | later       | the latest   |
| high   | higher      | the highest  |
| low    | lower       | the lowest   |
| near   | nearer      | the nearest  |

**Examples:**

- Mike worked **harder than** Sam (did).
- Sue worked **the hardest of** all.
- The bird flew **higher than** the plane.
- He hit the ball **harder than** me.

> **Exception:** early → earlier → earliest (change -y to -i)

### 3. Irregular adverbs

Some adverbs have irregular comparative and superlative forms.

| Adverb | Comparative     | Superlative       |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| badly  | worse           | worst             |
| far    | farther/further | farthest/furthest |
| little | less            | least             |
| well   | better          | best              |
| much   | more            | most              |

**Examples:**

- Rosa writes **better than** I do.
- Kim writes **the best of** all.
- The little boy ran **farther** than his friends.
- You're driving **worse** today than yesterday!

> **Note:** Better and best are forms of the adverb **well**.

### 4. Flat adverbs (formal vs. informal)

Some comparative and superlative adverbs have a **formal** and an **informal** form. An informal adverb drops the '-ly' suffix. These are also known as **flat adverbs**.

|**Adverb**|**Formal Comparative Adverb**|**Informal Comparative Adverb**|**Formal Superlative Adverb**|**Informal Superlative Adverb**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|quick/quickly|more quickly|quicker|most quickly|quickest|
|slow/slowly|more slowly|slower|most slowly|slowest|
|loud/loudly|more loudly|louder|most loudy|loudest|

> **Note:** Flat adverbs are generally seen as grammatically correct, but as they're informal, they shouldn't be used in formal writing, such as essays.

## Comparative of Equality: as...as

The structure **[as + adverb + as]** is used to express that two actions or states are **equal** in degree or intensity.

**Examples:**

- I work **as hard as** any other student in my class.
- Shyla cannot sing **as beautifully as** her sister.
- Tom walks **as slowly as** Sue walks.
- Today, the turtle ran **as fast as** he ran yesterday.

## Comparative of Minority: less

We can use **less** before the adverb to suggest a **lower degree** of the action or state.

**Examples:**

- Wilfred speaks **less loudly than** Jane.
- Electric scooters move **less quickly than** e-bikes move.
- Claire dances **less elegantly**.
- The engine operates **less efficiently** with alcohol.

> **Note:** Comparative adverbs with **less** are rare, and it is usually better to rephrase the sentence:
> - 🆗 *Wilfred speaks less loudly than Jane.*
> - ✅ *Jane speaks more loudly than Wilfred.*
> - ✅ *Wilfred does not speak as loudly as Jane.*

## Superlative of Minority: the least

We can use **the least** before the adverb to express the **lowest degree** of an action.

**Examples:**

- Pete acted **least sociably**.
- Karen moved **least gracefully of all**.
- This computer operates **the least efficiently**.
- Charles completed his homework **the least quietly**.

## Intensifiers and Mitigators

### Intensifiers with Comparatives

We can use these words and phrases to **intensify** comparative adverbs:

| Intensifier | Example |
|-------------|---------|
| much | I forget things **much more often** nowadays. |
| far | This is **far better**. |
| a lot | She works **a lot harder** now. |
| quite a lot | He speaks **quite a lot more** clearly. |
| a great deal | It's **a great deal more** complicated. |
| a good deal | She's **a good deal more** patient. |
| a good bit | It's **a good bit more** expensive. |
| a fair bit | He's **a fair bit** taller. |

### Mitigators with Comparatives

We can use these words and phrases to **mitigate** (soften) comparative adverbs:

| Mitigator | Example |
|-----------|---------|
| a bit | She began to speak **a bit more quickly**. |
| slightly | Could you speak **slightly more slowly**? |
| rather | He drives **rather more carefully** now. |
| a little | Can you walk **a little faster**? |
| a little bit | It's **a little bit** more difficult. |
| just a little bit | She's **just a little bit** taller. |

### Intensifiers with Superlatives

We can use these words and phrases to intensify superlative adverbs:

| Intensifier | Example |
|-------------|---------|
| easily | Of the three brothers, Brian **easily runs the fastest**. |
| by far | In our office, Jill works **by far the hardest**. |
| much | Company X has grown **much the fastest**. |

> **Note:** When we intensify a superlative adverb, we often put **the** in front of the adverb.

## Double Comparative/Superlative (Common Mistake)

It is important to avoid using a **double comparative** or **double superlative**.

| ❌ Incorrect | ✅ Correct |
|--------------|------------|
| He was running **the most fastest** out of all the racers. | He was running **the fastest** out of all the racers. |
| Of all the fish in Europe, pike attack **the most fastest**. | Of all the fish in Europe, pike attack **the fastest**. |

> **Rule:** Only use **one** comparative or superlative form at a time.

## Common Mistakes

| ❌ Incorrect | ✅ Correct | Explanation |
|--------------|------------|-------------|
| *Dave did the **littlest** amount of work.* | *Dave did the **least** amount of work.* | Use **least**, not "littlest". |
| *He speaks **more better** than me.* | *He speaks **better** than me.* | Don't use "more" with irregular forms. |
| *She runs **the most fastest**.* | *She runs **the fastest**.* | Don't use double superlatives. |
| *He works **more hard** than me.* | *He works **harder** than me.* | Use -er for one-syllable adverbs. |
| *She sings **beautiful** than me.* | *She sings **more beautifully** than me.* | Use adverb, not adjective. |
| *He talks **fastest** than me.* | *He talks **faster** than me.* | Use comparative for two things. |

## Summary Table

| Adverb Type | Comparative | Superlative |
|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| One syllable | adverb + **-er** | **the** adverb + **-est** |
| One syllable ending in **-e** | adverb + **-r** | **the** adverb + **-st** |
| Two or more syllables (-ly) | **more** + adverb | **the most** + adverb |
| Irregular (well) | better | the best |
| Irregular (badly) | worse | the worst |
| Irregular (far) | farther/further | farthest/furthest |
| Irregular (little) | less | least |
| Irregular (much) | more | most |
| Equality | **as** + adverb + **as** | — |
| Minority (comparative) | **less** + adverb | — |
| Minority (superlative) | — | **the least** + adverb |
