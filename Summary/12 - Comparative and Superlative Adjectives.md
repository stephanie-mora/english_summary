
# Comparative and Superlative Adjectives

## Introduction

We use **comparative adjectives** to compare **two** things:

> She is **more intelligent** than her sister.
> This is a **bigger** piece of cake.

We use **superlative adjectives** to compare **one** thing with **all others** of its kind:

> He is **the most intelligent** man I've ever met.
> This is **the biggest** piece of cake.

## Comparative Adjectives

**Comparative adjectives** are used to compare differences between **two objects** they modify (larger, smaller, faster, higher).

> **Structure:** **Noun (subject) + verb + comparative adjective + than + noun (object)**

**Examples**

- My house is **larger than** hers.
- This box is **smaller than** the one I lost.
- Your dog runs **faster than** Jim's dog.
- The rock flew **higher than** the roof.
- Jim and Jack are both my friends, but I like Jack **better**. (*than Jim* is understood)

> **Note:** The second item of comparison can be **omitted** if it is clear from the context.

### Uses of Comparatives

#### 1. To show change or make comparisons

- This car is certainly **better**, but it's **much more expensive**.
- I'm feeling **happier** now.
- We need a **bigger** garden.

#### 2. With "than" to compare one thing with another

- She is two years **older than** me.
- New York is **much bigger than** Boston.
- He is a **better** player **than** Ronaldo.
- France is a **bigger** country **than** Britain.

#### 3. With "and" to show continuous change

> **Comparative + and + comparative**

- The balloon got **bigger and bigger**.
- Everything is getting **more and more expensive**.
- Grandfather is looking **older and older**.

#### 4. With "the" to show one thing depends on another

> **The + comparative..., the + comparative...**

- **The faster** you drive, **the more dangerous** it is. (= When you drive faster, it is more dangerous.)
- **The higher** they climbed, **the colder** it got. (= When they climbed higher, it got colder.)

## Superlative Adjectives

**Superlative adjectives** are used to describe an object which is at the **upper or lower limit** of a quality (the tallest, the smallest, the fastest, the highest).
They are used in sentences where a **subject** is compared to a **group** of objects.

> **Structure:** **Subject + verb + the + superlative adjective + (group)**

**Examples**

- My house is **the largest** one in our neighborhood.
- This is **the smallest** box I've ever seen.
- Your dog ran **the fastest** of any dog in the race.
- We all threw our rocks at the same time. My rock flew **the highest**. (*of all the rocks* is understood)

> **Note:** The group that is being compared with can be **omitted** if it is clear from the context.

### Use of "the" with Superlatives

We use **the** with superlative adjectives:

- It was **the happiest** day of my life.
- Everest is **the highest** mountain in the world.
- That's **the best** film I have seen this year.
- I have three sisters: Jan is **the oldest** and Angela is **the youngest**.

## How to Form Comparatives and Superlatives

### 1. One-syllable adjectives

Add **-er** for comparative and **-est** for superlative.

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative  |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| old       | older       | oldest       |
| long      | longer      | longest      |
| soft      | softer      | the softest  |
| cheap     | cheaper     | the cheapest |
| sweet     | sweeter     | the sweetest |
| thin      | thinner     | the thinnest |
| big       | bigger      | the biggest  |

**Spelling rules:**

- If the adjective ends in **-e**, add **-r** or **-st**: *nice → nicer → nicest*, *large → larger → largest*
- If the adjective ends in a **vowel + consonant**, **double the consonant**: *big → bigger → biggest*, *fat → fatter → fattest*
- If the adjective ends in a **consonant + -y**, change **-y to -i** and add **-er/-est**: *happy → happier → happiest*, *silly → sillier → silliest*

### 2. More and most with one-syllable adjectives

**More** and **most** are sometimes used with one-syllable adjectives as an alternative to **-er/-est** when:

- We want to **emphasize** the comparison
- The adjective occurs with **another adjective** that has more than one syllable

**Examples:**

> The icing was supposed to be pink and white, but it looked **more red** than pink.
> That sofa might look nice, but this one is **more soft and comfortable**.

### 3. Two-syllable adjectives ending in **-y**

Add **-er** for comparative and **-est** for superlative. Change **-y** to **-i**.

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative   |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| lucky     | luckier     | the luckiest  |
| pretty    | prettier    | the prettiest |
| tidy      | tidier      | the tidiest   |
| happy     | happier     | happiest      |
| silly     | sillier     | silliest      |

### 4. Two-syllable adjectives ending in **-ed, -ing, -ful, -less**

Always use **more** for comparative and **the most** for superlative.

| Adjective | Comparative  | Superlative      |
|-----------|--------------|------------------|
| worried   | more worried | the most worried |
| boring    | more boring  | the most boring  |
| careful   | more careful | the most careful |
| useless   | more useless | the most useless |

### 5. Two-syllable adjectives (general rule)

Most other two-syllable adjectives also use **more/most**, except those ending in **-y**.

| Adjective   | Comparative      | Superlative      |
|-------------|------------------|------------------|
| careful     | more careful     | most careful     |
| interesting | more interesting | most interesting |

**However, some common two-syllable adjectives can use BOTH forms:**

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| common | commoner / more common | the commonest / most common |
| narrow | narrower / more narrow | the narrowest / most narrow |
| cruel | crueler / more cruel | the cruelest / most cruel |
| pleasant | pleasanter / more pleasant | the pleasantest / most pleasant |
| gentle | gentler / more gentle | the gentlest / most gentle |
| polite | politer / more polite | the politest / most polite |
| handsome | handsomer / more handsome | the handsomest / most handsome |
| simple | simpler / more simple | the simplest / most simple |
| likely | likelier / more likely | the likeliest / most likely |
| stupid | stupider / more stupid | the stupidest / most stupid |
| quiet | quieter / more quiet | the quietest / most quiet |

**Examples:**

> He is certainly **handsomer** than his brother.
> His brother is handsome, but he is **more handsome**.
> She is one of the **politest** people I have ever met.
> She is the **most polite** person I have ever met.

### 6. Three or more syllables

Always use **more** for comparative and **the most** for superlative.

| Adjective  | Comparative     | Superlative         |
|------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| dangerous  | more dangerous  | the most dangerous  |
| difficult  | more difficult  | the most difficult  |
| exciting   | more exciting   | the most exciting   |
| ridiculous | more ridiculous | the most ridiculous |

**Exception:** Some three-syllable adjectives formed with the prefix **un-** (especially from adjectives ending in **-y**) can use **both** forms:

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| unhappy | unhappier / more unhappy | the unhappiest / most unhappy |
| unfriendly | unfriendlier / more unfriendly | the unfriendliest / most unfriendly |

### 7. Irregular comparatives and superlatives

These very common adjectives have **completely irregular** comparative and superlative forms:

| Adjective | Comparative       | Superlative             |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------------|
| good      | better            | the best                |
| bad       | worse             | the worst               |
| little    | less              | least                   |
| much      | more              | most                    |
| far       | farther / further | the farthest / furthest |

#### Important Notes on Irregular Forms

**Ill and well (health):**
- The comparative of **ill** is **worse**.
- The comparative of **well** is **better**.

> She's feeling much **better/worse** today.

**Old, older, oldest vs. elder, eldest:**

| Form | Use | Example |
|------|-----|---------|
| **older / oldest** | General use (people and things) | It's the **oldest** castle in Britain. |
| **elder / eldest** | Only for **people**, especially **family members** | My **elder** brother. |

**Restrictions of elder/eldest:**

- **Elder/eldest** are used to talk about the **age of people**, especially within the **same family**.
- They are **NOT** used to talk about the age of **things**.
  - ✅ *It's the **oldest** castle in Britain.*
  - ❌ *It's the **eldest** castle in Britain.*
- **Elder** cannot occur after link verbs like **be, become, get**:
  - ✅ *We're all getting **older**.*
  - ❌ *We're all getting **elder**.*
  - ✅ *My brother is **older** than me.*
  - ❌ *My brother is **elder** than me.*

### 8. Compound adjectives

Comparatives and superlatives of compound adjectives are generally formed by using **more** and **most**.

> Going skiing was the **most nerve-wracking** experience I've had.

Some compound adjectives have a **first element** that is an adjective which would normally form a comparative/superlative in one word. These can form the comparative/superlative by changing the **first adjective**:

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| good-looking | better-looking | the best-looking |
| long-lasting | longer-lasting | the longest-lasting |
| low-paid | lower-paid | the lowest-paid |

### 9. Adjectives with already comparative/superlative meaning

Some adjectives already have a comparative or superlative meaning. These are **usually not used** with **-er/-est** or **more/most** unless for emphasis:

- complete
- equal
- favorite
- perfect

> *Mussels are my **most favorite** food.* (for emphasis/humorous effect)

**Common examples of adjectives like these are:** complete, equal, favorite, and perfect.

## Use of Comparatives

### Position

- **Before nouns** (attributive): *a **more intelligent** child*, *the **bigger** piece of cake*
- **After link verbs** (be, become, get): *The street has become **quieter** since they left.* *You should be **more sensible**.*

### With "than"

Comparatives are commonly followed by **than** + pronoun, noun group, or clause:

- John is **taller than** me.
- I think that she's **more intelligent than** her sister.
- I think the portions were **bigger than** they were last time.
- They had given a **better** performance **than** in previous years.

### Qualifying comparatives

| Word/Phrase | Example |
|-------------|---------|
| **much** | It would be **much cheaper**. |
| **a lot** | I'm feeling **a lot better**. |
| **far** | This is **far better**. |
| **a bit / a little** | Could you be **a bit quieter**? |
| **slightly** | Do you have one that's **slightly bigger**? |

### Two comparatives together

**The + comparative..., the + comparative...** (linked changes):

> **The smaller** the gift, **the easier** it is to send.
> **The more** stressed you are, **the worse** it is for your health.

### Continuous increase

**Comparative + and + comparative**:

> The sea was getting **rougher and rougher**.
> Her illness was becoming **worse and worse**.
> He became **more and more tired** as the weeks went by.

## Use of Superlatives

### Position

- **Before nouns** (attributive): *It is **the most delicious** chocolate cake I've ever eaten.*
- **After link verbs**: *Annabel was **the youngest**.* *This restaurant is **the best**.*

### Used alone

Superlatives are often used on their own if it is clear what or who is being compared:

> Annabel was **the youngest**.

### Being specific

Use a **noun**, or a phrase with **in** or **of**:

> Annabel was **the youngest child**.
> Annabel was **the youngest of the children**.
> This restaurant is **the best in town**.

### With relative clauses

> This offer is **the best I'm going to get**.
> 
> **Note:** If the superlative occurs before the noun (attributive), the **in/of phrase** or **relative clause** comes **after the noun**:
> - *The **best restaurant in town**.*
> - *The **best offer I'm going to get**.*

### Omitting "the" (informal)

**The** is sometimes left out in **informal** speech or writing:

> This one seems to be **cheapest**.
> 
> **Important:** **The** cannot be left out when the superlative is followed by an **of/in** phrase or a **relative clause**:
> - ✅ *This one is **the cheapest**.*
> - ✅ *This one is **cheapest**.* (informal)
> - ✅ *This one is **the cheapest of the new designs**.*
> - ❌ *This one is cheapest of the new designs.*
> - ✅ *This one is **the cheapest I could find**.*
> - ❌ *This one is cheapest I could find.*

### With possessive pronouns

Sometimes **possessive pronouns** are used instead of **the**:

> **my** youngest brother
> **her** most valuable piece of jewelry

### With ordinal numbers

Ordinal numbers are often used with superlatives:

> It's the **third largest** city in the country.
> The cathedral is the **second most popular** tourist attraction.

### Superlatives instead of comparatives (informal)

In **informal conversation**, superlatives are often used instead of comparatives when comparing **two** things:

> *The train is **quickest**.* (instead of: *The train is **quicker**.*)
> 
> **Note:** Superlatives are **not generally used** in this way in **formal** speech and writing.

## Less and The Least (Opposites)

| Form | Use | Example |
|------|-----|---------|
| **less** | Opposite of "more" | This sofa is **less comfortable**. |
| **the least** | Opposite of "the most" | It's **the least expensive** way to travel. |

**Examples:**

> I've always been **less patient** than my sister.
> She was **the least intelligent** of the three sisters.

## Summary Table

| Adjective Type | Comparative | Superlative |
|----------------|-------------|-------------|
| One syllable | adj + **-er** | **the** adj + **-est** |
| One syllable (emphasis) | **more** + adj | **the most** + adj |
| Two syllables ending in **-y** | adj + **-er** (-y → i) | **the** adj + **-est** |
| Two syllables (-ed, -ing, -ful, -less) | **more** + adj | **the most** + adj |
| Two syllables (general) | **more** + adj | **the most** + adj |
| Two syllables (some) | adj + **-er** / **more** + adj | **the** adj + **-est** / **the most** + adj |
| Three or more syllables | **more** + adj | **the most** + adj |
| Three syllables with **un-** | adj + **-er** / **more** + adj | **the** adj + **-est** / **the most** + adj |
| Irregular | special forms | special forms |
| Compound | **more** + adj / change first adj | **the most** + adj / change first adj |

## Key Points to Remember

1. **Comparatives** compare **two** things.
2. **Superlatives** compare **one** thing with **all others**.
3. Use **than** after comparatives.
4. Use **the** before superlatives.
5. Use **in** or **of** to specify the group.
6. **Irregular forms:** good → better → best, bad → worse → worst, far → farther/further → farthest/furthest, little → less → least, much → more → most.
7. **Elder/eldest** only for people (especially family), not things.
8. **Elder** cannot be used after **be, become, get**.
9. Use **less** and **the least** for the opposite meaning.
10. Some adjectives can use **both forms** (narrower/more narrow).
11. **Common two-syllable adjectives** can use both forms (common, narrow, cruel, pleasant, gentle, polite, handsome, simple, likely, stupid).
